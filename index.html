TL;DR

KiRoSe reads a robot's own end-effector kinematics, compressed into 2 Sparse Motion Tokens per second, and writes subtask boundaries and action descriptions with an open 9B language model. Trained on one robot (YAM), it transfers zero-shot to AgiBot G1 and human hands, beats frontier video models at locating boundaries on robot data, and costs thousands of times less per demonstration. The same tokenizer also tracks task progress and notices when an action is undone.

Read the abstract

Segmenting robot demonstration data into disjoint temporal and semantic subsegments is valuable for learning, cleaning, retrieval, and analysis. Segmentation is often performed manually in a tedious and error-prone process. Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have the potential to automate this process, but directly ingesting raw multi-camera video streams into MLLMs can be costly and can quickly exhaust context windows. We introduce KiRoSe (Kinematics for Robot Segmentation), a framework that focuses on the companion stream of robot kinematic data. Inspired by classical entropy coding such as Huffman coding, where frequent symbols get compact codes, we propose Sparse Motion Tokens (SMTs), a discrete kinematic vocabulary that compactly represents recurring, temporally localized motion patterns, which we call kinematic events. SMTs are learned by encoding kinematic trajectories with temporal-segment supervision and quantizing the resulting features. We use increasingly available robot demonstration subtask annotations to supervise an open language model to interpret the resulting token sequences. Evaluation against human-labeled segmentation shows that KiRoSe substantially outperforms baseline segmentation methods in F1 quality and transfers zero-shot to unseen robot embodiments and egocentric human motion, while preserving the language model's open-vocabulary knowledge. Beyond segmentation, the same frozen representation supports progress estimation: a small head trained only on YAM tracks task progress zero-shot on an unseen robot and detects when an action is being undone.

Sparse Motion Tokens

The same motion makes the same tokens. Recurring kinematic events, such as a bimanual lift, a handover, a pick or a place, map to recurring codes across tasks and objects.

Sparse Motion Tokens appear in sync with the robot (2 per second, from a 512-code vocabulary) on a shared time axis. When the clips end, lines join the tokens the two rows share in the same order (their longest common subsequence), and the shared sequence is spelled out: a single-arm pick-up is 438 → 456 → 445 → 431 → 296 in both tasks, and a single-arm place is 126 → 308 → 443 → 48 → 208. KiRoSe-SMT

The motion tokenizer: a 2.76M-parameter transition-supervised encoder and a 512-code vocabulary. It maps any end-effector trajectory to discrete tokens and features.

KiRoSe-Reader

Qwen3.5-9B with LoRA. It reads motion tokens and the task instruction and writes timed subtasks with action descriptions.

KiRoSe-Progress

A 68k-parameter head on the tokenizer's features that outputs dense, signed task progress and detects when an action is being undone.

Method

KiRoSe has two stages. A kinematic encoder is trained with subtask-transition supervision, and its features are pooled and quantized into Sparse Motion Tokens. The tokenizer is then frozen while a pretrained language model learns to read the tokens and write segments.

Shared end-effector input

Every embodiment is mapped to the same 14 channels at 30 Hz: per arm or hand, linear velocity, angular velocity and gripper opening rate. For robots these come from end-effector motion and gripper aperture; for human hands, from wrist motion, palm orientation and thumb–index separation.

xt = ⊕h∈{L,R} [ vh,t , ωh,t , ġh,t ] ∈ ℝ14

Transition-supervised temporal encoder

Residual temporal convolutions map the stream to contextual features. During training, an auxiliary head predicts a transition probability at every step against soft targets centred on annotated subtask boundaries, with a focal binary cross-entropy loss. The head is discarded afterwards; the 2.76M-parameter encoder is kept.

Sparse Motion Tokens

Encoder features are pooled on a uniform grid and assigned to the nearest of K = 512 k-means centroids fitted on training trajectories only. Token positions do not depend on annotated or predicted boundaries; supervision shapes what the features encode, not where tokens fall.

Language-model reader

Qwen3.5-9B receives 512 new vocabulary entries, <k0> to <k511>, plus textual time markers every second and the task instruction. LoRA adaptation trains it to emit the reference segmentation as JSON with start time, end time and a description per segment. The tokenizer stays frozen, so the model learns to read the motion vocabulary rather than reshape it.

Cross-Embodiment Transfer

The encoder, codebook and reader are trained on YAM only. On AgiBot G1 and human hands they run zero-shot through the shared end-effector representation.

  • Timing transfers. KiRoSe reaches 0.40 Boundary F1 ±0.5 s on all 3,852 AgiBot episodes without AgiBot data, against 0.48 on held-out YAM. The video Qwen3.5-9B baseline reaches 0.19 on the 357 AgiBot episodes with video.
  • Ahead of a frontier video model. On 20 matched AgiBot episodes, GPT-6 Astra reaches 0.30 and KiRoSe 0.61.
  • The motion signal is used. Removing the motion tokens, or swapping in tokens from another trajectory, drops Boundary F1 ±1 s from 0.61–0.63 to 0.31–0.35 on both robots.
  • Actions transfer, object names need vision. On the matched AgiBot episodes, action-level Grounded F1 is 0.593 for KiRoSe and 0.349 for Astra; on full descriptions with object names Astra leads (0.35 vs 0.23 embedding).

Unseen-task vocabulary on human hands

On Ego-Exo4D the reader sees only the task name and motion tokens. A supplied word appears in that task's human annotations but never in YAM training data or the prompt. Such words occur in 6 of 12 tasks, which indicates that YAM adaptation keeps the language model's open vocabulary.

Key Findings

  1. Kinematics carry the boundaries. Even reconstruction tokenizers (FAST, OAT) beat a zero-shot video model at locating subtask transitions, and transition-supervised SMTs raise Boundary F1 ±1 s from about 0.42 to 0.63.
  2. Transition supervision transfers across bodies. With only rates in a shared end-effector space, a YAM-trained model keeps most of its boundary accuracy on AgiBot G1 (0.40 vs 0.48 at ±0.5 s) and runs on human hands.
  3. Motion gives the action; vision gives the object. KiRoSe leads GPT-6 Astra on action-level Grounded F1 on AgiBot, and trails on descriptions that need object names.
  4. The vocabulary stays open. On unseen human tasks the reader writes task-appropriate words such as “swab” and “whisk” that never appear in its YAM training annotations.
  5. The representation encodes direction. Frozen SMT-encoder features let a small probe tell forward execution from an action being undone on an unseen robot, where video reward models keep counting up.
