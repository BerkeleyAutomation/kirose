Sparse Motion Tokens

The same motion makes the same tokens. Recurring kinematic events, such as a bimanual lift, a handover, a pick or a place, map to recurring codes across tasks and objects.

How a Sparse Motion Token is made

From motion to tokens. ① End-effector kinematics (14 channels at 30 Hz). ② A dilated temporal convolution encoder: each layer doubles its dilation, so one output feature sees seconds of motion around it (highlighted). ③ During training only, a head on these features predicts where annotated subtasks change (focal loss on soft targets around each boundary); afterwards the head is dropped and the encoder is frozen. ④ Features are pooled to 2 per second and snapped to the nearest of 512 k-means centroids. ⑤ The resulting token stream, plus the task instruction, is what the Qwen3.5-9B reader sees. Illustration: 3 layers with kernel 3; the real encoder has 7 blocks with kernel 5 and dilations 1–64 (17 s receptive field, 2.76M parameters).

Architecture

KiRoSe has two stages. A kinematic encoder is trained with subtask-transition supervision, and its features are pooled and quantized into Sparse Motion Tokens. The tokenizer is then frozen while a pretrained language model learns to read the tokens and write segments.

Shared end-effector input

Every embodiment is mapped to the same 14 channels at 30 Hz: per arm or hand, linear velocity, angular velocity and gripper opening rate. For robots these come from end-effector motion and gripper aperture; for human hands, from wrist motion, palm orientation and thumb–index separation.

xt = ⊕h∈{L,R} [ vh,t , ωh,t , ġh,t ] ∈ ℝ14

Transition-supervised temporal encoder

Residual temporal convolutions map the stream to contextual features. During training, an auxiliary head predicts a transition probability at every step against soft targets centred on annotated subtask boundaries, with a focal binary cross-entropy loss. The head is discarded afterwards; the 2.76M-parameter encoder is kept.

Sparse Motion Tokens

Encoder features are pooled on a uniform grid and assigned to the nearest of K = 512 k-means centroids fitted on training trajectories only. Token positions do not depend on annotated or predicted boundaries; supervision shapes what the features encode, not where tokens fall.

Language-model reader

Qwen3.5-9B receives 512 new vocabulary entries, <k0> to <k511>, plus textual time markers every second and the task instruction. LoRA adaptation trains it to emit the reference segmentation as JSON with start time, end time and a description per segment. The tokenizer stays frozen, so the model learns to read the motion vocabulary rather than reshape it.

Cross-Embodiment Transfer

The encoder, codebook and reader are trained on YAM only. On AgiBot G1 and human hands they run zero-shot through the shared end-effector representation.

  • Timing transfers. KiRoSe reaches 0.40 Boundary F1 ±0.5 s on all 3,852 AgiBot episodes without AgiBot data, against 0.48 on held-out YAM. The video Qwen3.5-9B baseline reaches 0.19 on the 357 AgiBot episodes with video.
  • Ahead of a frontier video model. On 20 matched AgiBot episodes, GPT-6 Astra reaches 0.30 and KiRoSe 0.61.
  • The motion signal is used. Removing the motion tokens, or swapping in tokens from another trajectory, drops Boundary F1 ±1 s from 0.61–0.63 to 0.31–0.35 on both robots.
  • Actions transfer, object names need vision. On the matched AgiBot episodes, action-level Grounded F1 is 0.593 for KiRoSe and 0.349 for Astra; on full descriptions with object names Astra leads (0.35 vs 0.23 embedding).

Summary

KiRoSe shows that a robot's own end-effector kinematics are enough to segment its demonstrations. Compressed into two Sparse Motion Tokens per second, kinematics locate subtask boundaries better than a zero-shot video model, and transition supervision raises Boundary F1 ±1 s from about 0.42 to 0.63. Because the tokens live in a shared end-effector space, a model trained only on YAM keeps most of its boundary accuracy on AgiBot G1 and runs on human hands, while its open-vocabulary reader writes task words it never saw in training. Motion supplies the action and vision the object: KiRoSe leads GPT-6 Astra on action-level Grounded F1 and trails it where descriptions need object names. The same frozen encoder also tells forward execution from an action being undone, which video reward models miss, all at a small fraction of the cost of video-based segmentation.
